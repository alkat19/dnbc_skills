# Sediment Logic

**A movement concerned with what accumulates, and what the accumulation proves.**

---

Sediment Logic begins from a single conviction: that duration leaves residue, and residue
can be read. Where other movements chase the gesture — the sweep, the flourish, the mark
made once and quickly — this one attends to the deposit. A thing observed a thousand times
is not a thing observed once, multiplied. It is a different kind of object entirely. The
work must therefore be built from repetition rather than decorated with it, and the
repetition must be exact: identical units, laid down with the patience of a hand that has
done this for years and expects to do it for years more. Nothing here is gestural. Every
mark is deliberate, placed, and countable, and the composition earns its authority from
the sheer evident labour of that placement.

**Form and space.** The governing structure is the band — the stratum — and its logic is
accretion. The oldest deposit lies at the core; what is recent forms around what came
before, never displacing it.
Bands are never equal, because the periods they record were never equal, and the work must
refuse the comfort of even division. A stratum's thickness is an argument. Space between
strata is not decoration but the boundary between one condition and another, and a
boundary earns a line only when something genuinely changed. Emptiness is permitted, even
required, at the edges: the record does not extend infinitely, and honest work shows where
observation began and where it stopped. The margin is not leftover room. It is the
statement that the record has limits.

**Colour and material.** A single family of tone, descending. The palette is that of ink
on good paper, aged toward grey — nothing saturated, nothing that would read as opinion.
Against this restraint, exactly one chromatic event is permitted across the entire
composition, and it must fall on the moment that matters most. One line, one hue, one
occurrence. A second use would halve the meaning of the first. The surface should feel
like pressed paper and dry pigment: matte, absorbed, without gloss or glow. This is a
material that has been handled.

**Scale and rhythm.** The unit mark is small — small enough that at conversational
distance the eye reads texture and tonal mass, not individual marks. Only on approach does
the grain resolve into discrete, countable things, and this second reading is the work's
reward. The lattice must be regular to a tolerance the eye cannot fault: rows true,
spacing constant, offsets exact. A single misplaced unit destroys the illusion of
systematic observation, and with it the entire premise. This demands painstaking
calibration and admits no shortcut.

**Composition and hierarchy.** The silhouette does the first work, and it must read
whole and unmistakable from across a room. Within it, hierarchy runs: mass, then boundary,
then name. Typography arrives last and smallest — a single word, set in a face built for
instruments rather than for persuasion, letter-spaced with the care of an engraved plate,
and given clean ground to sit on. It never overlaps the record it labels. It never
competes. It is the tag tied to the specimen, not the specimen.

**On execution.** This philosophy is unforgiving of approximation. Its entire effect
depends on the viewer's sense that someone counted — that the proportions are not
approximated for visual convenience but derived, that the boundaries fall where the
evidence puts them, that the maker refused to nudge a line for prettiness. The result
should look as though it cost many hours and considerable expertise, because a work about
patient accumulation that was not itself patiently accumulated is a lie its own form
exposes. Master-level execution is not a finish applied at the end. It is the only way the
argument holds.
